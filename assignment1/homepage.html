The History of the Internet

Originally starting as a Cold War military experiment, the internet is now a global network connecting billions of people and billions of machines.

1960s: The Concept Emerges

The roots of the internet can be traced back to the early 1960s when computer scientists like Paul Baran, Donald Davies and Leonard Kleinrock developed packet-switching theory. This process broke data into small packets that could be moved and routed across networks, completely unlike traditional circuit-switching telephone systems. Baran’s RAND Corporation envisioned a communications network that could survive any nuclear attack, becoming the foundation for ARPANET

1969: ARPANET Goes Live

On October 29, 1969, the first ARPANET link connected UCLA’s Interface Message Processor to another machine at the Stanford Research Institute. A student named Charley Kline sent the very first message: “LOGIN.” The system crashed after “LO” but this still marked the birth of the internet. By December, ARPANET connected four nodes: UCLA, Stanford, UC Santa Barbara and the University of Utah. This network diagram from December 1969 shows the very first ‘map’ of the internet.

Through the 1970s, ARPANET expanded and its researchers tackled various problems with the system. Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn developed the TCP/IP protocols, which are still used on the internet today. In 1971, electronic mail was invented by Ray Tomlinson. In 1973, ARPANET served about 200 hosts all over the country. International connections finally began with connections to London and Norway.

January 1, 1983 was when the TCP/IP protocol was finally ready, unifying all the unlinked networks into one. The Domain Name System or DNS launched in the same year, replacing IP addresses with human readable URLs. The National Science Foundation Network started in 1985 and connected supercomputer centres to become a major academic backbone for the early internet.

1990s: World Wide Web Revolution

At CERN, Tim Berners-Lee wrote a proposal in March 1989 for what he called a “universal linked information system” that would become the World Wide Web. He created the first version of HTML, HTTP and URLs. The very first website went online at http://info.cern.ch in 1991. Tim had made these web technologies freely available so it was much easier to adopt them worldwide.

In 1993 the Mosaic browser was born, which introduced a user-friendly way to browse the internet through graphics. The number of web pages grew from a few in 1993 to tens of thousands within a year.

Commercial Explosion: 1995–2000

Dial‑up internet providers such as AOL and CompuServe connected millions of home users in the mid‑1990s. E‑commerce pioneers like Amazon and eBay showed that online shopping could be mainstream. JavaScript enabled interactive web pages in the browser. By around the year 2000, roughly half of households in some developed countries had internet access, and broadband technologies such as DSL and cable modems started to replace dial‑up.

2000s: Web 2.0 and Social Media

In the 2000s, Web 2.0 emphasised user‑generated content, collaboration, and dynamic websites. Blogging platforms, Wikipedia, and social networks like Facebook and YouTube transformed web users into active contributors to the system. Twitter, launched in 2006, majorly popularised short‑form real‑time communication, while the introduction of smartphones brought the web with people through their daily lives.

Cloud computing also emerged as a major trend, with services such as Amazon Web Services providing on‑demand computing power over the internet. At the same time, IPv6 was introduced to replace IPv4 to support vastly more connected devices.

2010s–Present: Mobile, Streaming, AI

By the 2010s, smartphones had become the primary way many people accessed the internet, and mobile traffic exceeded desktop traffic. Streaming services like Netflix and Spotify replaced much of the old download‑based or physical media model, relying on fast broadband and content delivery networks. Social media platforms became major sources of news, communication, and advertising.

High‑speed 4G and then 5G mobile networks enabled better applications, including real‑time gaming, ultra‑HD video, and low‑latency services. Global internet usage climbed to over half the world's population by the mid‑2020s, meaning billions of users were online every day. Large‑scale artificial intelligence systems and cloud APIs integrated more deeply into everyday apps and services, making the internet not just a network of pages but a platform for these intelligent services.
